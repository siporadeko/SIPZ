Finding a Standard:
My Reflection on the ADH Project

Designing to Repair the World

The motto "Design to Repair the World" really hit home for me during this project. Before starting this, I never really thought about how a simple food delivery could be a barrier, but when you look at the Brandeis campus, the "built environment" is actually broken for a lot of people. When a delivery driver leaves a bag on the floor, they are accidentally creating a hazard for someone with a visual impairment or a physical obstacle for someone who can’t bend down. My goal wasn't just to build a piece of furniture, but to create a "repair" for that social breakdown. I wanted to design a standardized interface that gives students their independence and dignity back.

Defining and Measuring Success

We didn't want to just guess if our design worked, so we set up two main ways to measure success. First, we looked at visibility. We tested the sign at 8:30 PM to see if a low-vision user could actually find the hub in the dark. Second, we measured tactile efficiency. We timed how long it took blindfolded users to feel the sign and understand the "Drop Here" instructions. We were aiming for a significant increase in visibility and a recognition time of under ten seconds.
The Result: Our final validation at 8:30 PM showed an average visibility of 20.6 feet and a tactile recognition time of 7.2 seconds with a 100% identification rate.

The Engineering Pivot: What Actually Worked?

If you asked me at the start, I would have said the CAD layout or the laser settings were the most important parts. But after four cycles of prototyping, it became clear that the material choice was the real game-changer. Our early wood prototypes basically disappeared in the dark, and stickers didn't have the right feel for touch-based navigation. When we finally switched to laser-etched acrylic with high-contrast vinyl, the results were night and day. We saw a huge jump in how far away people could see the sign compared to our initial wooden baseline. This taught me that as an engineer, you can have a perfect digital drawing, but if you pick the wrong material for the real world, the design fails the user.

It’s Never Really Finished

One thing I’ve realized is that no matter how much we perfected this version, there is always more prototyping to do. Even with our success in Cycle 4, I’m still thinking about how to make it more weather-proof or how to make the instructions even more intuitive. Design is a constant loop of learning and fixing. Because of that, I’m really hoping to keep working on this over the summer. I want to move past the prototype stage and actually get these hubs implemented in spots like Usdan so they can become a real, permanent part of campus life.
